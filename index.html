I was born and brought up in Bihar, India. I study the science of quantum information, asking how it is represented, manipulated, protected, and transmitted, so we can build better quantum computing tools. In my free time, I compose poems in Hindi and enjoy travelling. You can find my academic CV here.

Since 2019, I have been a theoretical physics student. Then, over the past few years (onwards from 2025), I used to perceive physics and information science as two very separate subjects, but gradually, the gap started diminishing, as if someone had been waiting for you to show up. To me, they are indeed two remarkably self-consistent worlds, each with its own language, questions, mathematical backbone, and ways of looking at reality. But the really interesting things seem to happen when we, humans, for our own fulfilment of desires, try hard enough to sense a connection between these two worlds and seek the existence of overlapping regimes. One may wonder what connects physics and information; I regard that as entropy. A quantity that appears naturally when we talk about uncertainty and information also appears at the heart of statistical mechanics and thermodynamics. Entropy becomes a kind of bridge between what we know about a system and what the physical system itself is doing.

We know physics tries to understand how nature works, how physical systems behave, what laws govern them, how to measure quantities, or how to predict dynamics. It’s mostly about unveiling hidden connections and then building blocks to exploit its predictability for our welfare. Now, information science looks at another side of the same picture, with added flavour, by first linking itself to the former by saying that “Information is a physical quantity” (this is a grand realization), and then grabbing similar tools as a physics researcher does, like how it can be represented or encoded, transformed, but then something unique happens: it also asks how information is stored, communicated, compressed, and recovered, and suddenly, unlike physics, its talk has seemingly less about predictability and representation but more about realizing or doing, particularly whenever they meet at a place. This overlapping zone is one of the directions in modern research and is a broad area of my interest.

This is where one may encounter classical and digital computing, continuous-variable computing, quantum computing, quantum communication and networks, neuromorphic computing, and many other ways of processing information. They may look like very different technologies, but underneath them is a common question: what can we do with information, and what does the physical world allow us to do with it? Each approach brings its own physics, mathematics, possibilities, limitations, and problems. Quantum computing is a good example. We are not simply trying to build a faster computer. We are trying to understand how quantum systems (photons, superconductors, atoms, ions, etc.) can be controlled, how information can be encoded in them, how noise can be fought through quantum error correction and mitigation schemes, how fault-tolerant computation can be achieved, and how individual quantum systems can eventually become part of larger quantum networks or provide security. At the laboratory level, all of this comes down to research domains of quantum matter, simulation, metrology-sensing, control theory, even manufacturing, electronics, etc. Here, my interest focuses on gaining skills or experiences in quantum computing and error correction to be able to have a fault-tolerant system. But there is another layer that I find equally interesting. Once we accept that information is physical, we can start asking much deeper questions. Why does nature allow quantum information to behave the way it does? Why does entanglement exist at all? Why is it so fundamentally different from anything we encounter in classical information? And more generally, what does it actually mean for information to exist in a physical universe? This is what a quantum foundation is supposed to sound like, but it generally does not pay well enough to sustain a competitive academic career. While I mostly admire natural philosophy, no one wants to be an investigating philosopher.

Information theory, the other side of the picture, opens an equally rich mathematical world, which I find somewhat interesting. It captures algorithms, computational complexity, coding and decoding, compression, memory, storage, and data recovery, all having their own structures and problems. And behind these structures lies the mathematics of tensor networks, algebras, topology, computational physics, geometry, group or Lie theories, and many other ideas that give us the language to describe transformations, symmetries, states, and information-processing systems. For me, it seems to be a less comforting domain, but rightfully a highly relevant one for the methods of research I do.

At the end, we can agree on why one may find the intersection so compelling. It is not only about inventing a new kind of computer or making an existing technology better. It is about understanding the physical limits of computation, finding ways to store and communicate information reliably, dealing with noise and uncertainty, extracting information through measurement, simulating nature, and discovering what new forms of information processing might be possible. And perhaps the most interesting part is that the questions go in both directions. Information gives us a new way to think about physical systems, while physics tells us what information can actually do. Mathematics provides the structure that connects the two, and technology is where many of these ideas eventually become tangible. So, for me, the marriage of physics and information science is much bigger than any single field such as quantum computing. And I think, hopefully, to be a better information physicist, let’s see how things unfold.
